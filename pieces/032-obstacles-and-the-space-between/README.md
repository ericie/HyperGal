# Obstacles, and The Space Between

A dry-garden companion to **Obstacles and How to Avoid Them** and
**Obstacles and How to Go Around Them**.

The piece looks straight down onto a rectangular karesansui garden. Its stones
inherit the plan-view language of the petals study: irregular survey-like
footprints, a quiet gray-beige fill, dark outlines, two nested contours, sparse
flecks, and small specimen numbers. Fine paired lines rake the remaining gravel
into samon, yielding as they meet each stone and returning slowly to the open
field.

The floating panel exposes the rules that compose the garden. Kisū limits the
stone count to an odd five, seven, or nine. Ma expands the intervals between
groups. Fukinsei pulls the arrangement away from bilateral balance.
Sanzon-seki turns subordinate stones into unequal triangles around a dominant
stone. Shizen lets each footprint's proportions govern its orientation and
irregularity. Shakkei carries an isolated stone through the edge of the frame,
implying that the garden continues beyond what can be seen. Samon interval and
current control the density of the rake marks and how strongly they turn around
the stones.

The composition draws on the dry landscape as a miniature natural world, the
historic use of asymmetric odd-numbered stone arrangements, and the Japanese
garden practice of arranging groups in relationship to one another rather than
as isolated objects. The current is an interpretation for this browser piece,
not a claim that every historic dry garden uses the same rake pattern.

Click or tap the gravel, or press `R`, Space, or Enter, to generate a new garden.
The panel can be dragged by its header or collapsed. Add `?seed=<value>` to the
URL for a repeatable starting composition.

Open `index.html` directly in any modern browser. The artwork has no runtime
dependencies.
