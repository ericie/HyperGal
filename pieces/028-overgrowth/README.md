# Overgrowth

A directionless botanical wallpaper drawn entirely in Canvas 2D. Roses, peonies, poppies, tiger lilies, hibiscus, chrysanthemums, daisies, anemones, mophead hydrangeas, fringed Iris japonica, dwarf quince, honeysuckle, acanthus, green palm and fern foliage, and cream acanthus, rubber tree, Fatsia japonica, palm, and fern leaves are engraved onto aged rag paper in dye-pot colour — madder, weld, woad, walnut — with contour lines, curved hatch shading, turnover leaves, recurved prickles, and coiling tendrils. A faded silhouette underprint sits behind the growth, a ruled border frames the sheet, and a few specimens overgrow it.

The piece is laid down in three passes. First the plate: a very dense wallpaper covering the whole sheet, drawn edge to edge. Then the mount: a white board with a window cut in it, bevelled at the cut and throwing a shadow down onto the artwork it hides. Then the overgrowth proper — every plant that follows is rooted inside that window, most of them against its rim and aimed straight out of it, so the only direction left for the garden to spread is out over the board.

The plate and the mount are laid in the same impression. Nothing is ever shown before the board is in place: the window is the start of visibility, and the wallpaper is only ever seen through it. There is no caption, no plate number, no text of any kind on the sheet.

A window is any set of closed contours, some of them holes, so the same machinery cuts a triangle, a square, a circle, or a word. Each plate takes a geometric window two times in five and a word the rest of the time. The words are ones the growth can either make good on or bury, and both readings are the piece: GROW, WILD, FERAL, BLOOM, SEED, WEED, THORN, EDEN, HOPE, LOVE, STILL, QUIET, STAY, GONE, RUIN, DECAY, MOURN, FEAR, DIE, VOTE, RIOT, BURN, FUCK.

They are set in a heavy square face cut as outlines on a unit box — a 0.30 vertical stem against a lighter 0.22 horizontal bar, counters carried as holes, diagonals only where a letter cannot do without them — and the whole word is scaled to the sheet. The face covers A to Z, so `?frame=` takes any word at all as well as any of the three shape names. Because the contours can be concave and pierced, a root is found by rejection sampling against the window's own path rather than by fanning from a centre, and a specimen is aimed along the outward normal of the nearest edge — which is measured once, at build time, by probing just off each edge to see which side is not window.

Nothing in the garden grows straight or observes an upright. Every stem, branch, and vine is built by the same curve walker, then turned into an all-angle repeat. The walker accumulates a guaranteed net turn plus a sinusoidal sway along its length; leaf blades bow on a curved rib, tendrils are logarithmic spirals, and even the border rule is drawn with a hand's wobble.

The first impression is deliberately dense and flower-rich, with extra blooms distributed across the repeat before the endless growth begins. Large off-white foliage prints late in the composition, varying the negative space between broad oval rubber leaves, seven-lobed Fatsia leaves, long feathered palm pinnae, and dense tapered fern fronds. Fresh specimens start roughly twice as often while keeping their measured individual growth duration: stems extend along their curves, branches wait for the main stem to reach their nodes, and leaves and flowers each pass through twenty drawn developmental poses. The pose grid is applied once, to raw progress: snapping a value that has already been snapped and then rescaling it onto a staged part's own window silently merges poses, and the parts furthest down a stagger lose the most. A palm frond's outermost pinnae were animating in thirteen poses rather than twenty, which is what made the largest leaf in the piece the choppiest. Leaf ribs lengthen before their blades broaden and fan away from the stem. Terminal buds flatten, sepals hinge back, petals lengthen and separate, and the flower centre appears last. Finished plants are stamped over everything already on the canvas forever. The pixels accumulate, but completed plant descriptions do not, so the endless growth stays memory-bounded.

Because every root stays in the window, reach is what carries the growth outward, and later specimens are given more of it so the mount is taken a little more of at a time rather than the colony stalling at one radius. Roots are placed around the rim on a golden-ratio walk, so every flank of the window is colonised at the same rate — with a word, that means all four letters at once.

Off-white foliage is kept sparse and printed early. It was planted negative space when the surround was tan paper; against a white board it reads as another hole in the plate, so the growth is left to cover it.

Resizing or requesting a new arrangement draws a fresh plate under a fresh window — a different shape as often as not — and begins filling up again.
